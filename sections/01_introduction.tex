\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Pricing a generative-AI service is not only a static problem of allocating usage. Enterprise users can invest in workflow redesign, data connections, internal tools, or task-specific routines before the provider chooses the terms that govern later use. Once those investments are sunk, the provider faces an installed base whose composition depends on what users expected the provider to do. This creates a feedback problem: anticipated tariff architecture changes integration, while the resulting integration state changes the provider's subsequent incentive to choose that architecture.

This paper isolates that feedback in a deliberately small model. A monopoly provider serves two classes of potential enterprise task integrations. Each task decides whether to incur a heterogeneous sunk integration cost. Integrated tasks then face an anonymous two-part tariff $T(q)=F+pq$. We call $p=0$ the flat architecture and $p>0$ the metered/hybrid architecture. Activating metering requires a real fixed cost $\mu$. The provider cannot commit before integration to its complete future tariff schedule. After observing the installed masses, it chooses the architecture and then optimizes the tariff parameters within that architecture.

The central result is a separation between a static tariff threshold and dynamic self-consistency thresholds. On a certified regular region in which both task classes are globally served by the relevant continuation tariffs, anticipated metering induces a lower installed mass of high-use integrations than anticipated flat pricing. Let these states be $h_M$ and $h_F$, respectively. The provider's gross gain from metering at installed state $(l,h)$ is $\Phi(l,h)$, and $\Phi$ is strictly increasing in $h$. Hence
\[
 h_M<h_F \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mu_M\equiv\Phi(l,h_M)<\mu_F\equiv\Phi(l,h_F).
\]
For $\mu<\mu_M$, the metered pure expectation is self-consistent on the regular branch; for $\mu>\mu_F$, the flat pure expectation is self-consistent. For the strict intermediate interval $\mu_M<\mu<\mu_F$, neither pure expectation is self-consistent. The regular branch is instead resolved by a unique mixed architecture. This is the paper's main mechanism: the architecture changes the state on which its own ex-post profitability is evaluated.

The benchmark in which integration is exogenous clarifies what is doing the work. If the installed state does not depend on anticipated architecture, then $h_M=h_F$ and the two thresholds collapse to one. Thus the result is not a restatement of the static fact that two-part tariffs can outperform flat pricing, nor is it simply the familiar observation that lack of commitment can discourage investment. The distinctive object is the reciprocal loop from expected tariff architecture to integration and back to the provider's architecture incentive.

The welfare analysis is intentionally narrower than the equilibrium result. Holding the installed base fixed, the provider's private metering threshold exceeds the threshold of a social evaluator comparing the same decentralized allocations by the exact amount $d h p^*(h)>0$. This establishes a fixed-allocation private/social wedge. Once integration is allowed to respond endogenously to architecture, however, the welfare ranking is not sign-definite in the baseline family, so the paper does not claim that metering is generically excessive or that either architecture is always welfare superior.

The paper connects several literatures without claiming novelty for their individual components. Nonlinear pricing of information goods already studies fixed fees, usage charges, and transaction costs \citep{sundararajan2004}. Dynamic subscription pricing studies commitment and adoption by forward-looking customers \citep{penmetsa2015}. Platform and application-pricing models show that sunk participation can create hold-up and that contract form can affect participation \citep{gans2012,mutherswismer2022}. Recent work on AI and cloud services studies token costs, two-part tariffs, committed spend, endogenous quality, and costly digital-service consumption \citep{bergemannbonattismolin2025,bergemannwang2025,bhaskaran2026,bichuchyaish2026}. Relative to these contributions, our claim is deliberately narrow: architecture-sensitive sunk integration can split a later tariff-switching condition into distinct self-consistency thresholds.

Two scope restrictions matter throughout. First, the headline theorems are branch-specific. Outside the regular both-served region, another continuation active set can dominate; an exact retained counterexample shows that an H-only flat tariff can beat the both-served flat candidate. Second, the broader robustness statement is a sufficient-condition result based on ordered architecture-induced states and an increasing metering-gain map, not a theorem for arbitrary concave demand. These restrictions are part of the economic content rather than technical footnotes.
